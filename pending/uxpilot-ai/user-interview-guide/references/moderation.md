# Moderation

## Contents

- Session preparation, roles, and opening
- Conduct during the conversation
- Common moderator failures
- Remote-session boundaries and closing

## Before the session

- Pilot the guide with a colleague or representative participant.
- Confirm timing, recording, note-taking, incentives, accessibility, and technology.
- Know the decision and must-cover questions well enough not to read mechanically.
- Put the team's hypotheses in moderator notes, never in the participant script.
- Separate research from sales, support, and product education whenever possible.

## Team roles and observers

- Assign one lead interviewer. Avoid passing the conversation among several people.
- Assign a note-taker or timekeeper when available so the interviewer can listen and follow up.
- Tell the participant who is observing and why. Keep observers silent unless their role was explained.
- Agree on the note structure, recording boundaries, must-cover questions, and post-call debrief owner before the session.
- Collect observer questions privately and ask only the few that still matter near the end.

## Opening

Use plain language:

> Thanks for speaking with me. We are learning about [topic] so we can make better decisions about [general purpose]. There are no right answers, and candid experiences are useful. Participation is voluntary, and you can skip a question or stop at any time. Are you comfortable taking part? What questions do you have before we begin?

If recording:

> Before I start recording, do I have your permission to record this session for [named audience and purpose]?

Wait for an explicit yes before recording. If the participant declines, follow the approved no-recording fallback (usually interviewer notes), or stop if recording is genuinely required and that condition was disclosed during recruitment.

Do not promise anonymity, confidentiality, deletion, or limited access unless the actual research process supports it.

## During the conversation

- Start with an easy, relevant question.
- Use silence. Do not rescue the participant from every pause.
- Use neutral acknowledgements such as “I see” or “Tell me more,” not “Great” or “Exactly.”
- Treat the guide as flexible: change order, skip covered prompts, and pursue relevant surprises without changing the research objective.
- Follow the participant's language; do not replace it with product terminology.
- Ask for a recent event, then move chronologically.
- Request an artifact or demonstration only with consent and a safe way to hide sensitive information.
- Clarify contradictions without cross-examining: “Earlier I heard X, and now Y. Help me understand how those fit together.”
- If the participant asks how the product works, defer explanation until research questions are complete.
- If they give a feature request, ask about the last situation that prompted it.
- If they generalize, ask for the most recent example.
- If they become uncomfortable, offer to skip or stop.

## Common moderator failures

### Stacking questions

Do not ask a core question and several probes in one breath. Ask one question, listen to the complete answer, then choose the single follow-up that matters most.

### Interrupting and filling silence

Do not complete the participant's sentence, answer your own question, or rescue every pause. Silence often produces the detail that a rapid follow-up would suppress.

### Signaling a preferred answer

Do not praise a desired response, show disappointment, or use reactions such as “Exactly” and “That's great.” Use neutral acknowledgement and stay curious about disconfirming evidence.

### Seeding language and options

Do not list the team's hypotheses, features, benefits, competitors, or possible reasons before asking for unaided recall. Ask openly first; introduce a specific item later only when the research requires it.

### Rigidly following the guide

Do not ask every written probe, repeat a question already answered, or cut off a useful story because the next section is scheduled. Protect the objective and must-cover questions, not the exact script.

### Inflating evidence

Do not treat compliments, generalities, feature requests, remembered averages, or future promises as observed behavior. Do not turn one participant's account into a prevalence claim. Record what was said or observed separately from interpretation.

### Crossing boundaries

Do not request passwords, payment details, customer-confidential material, or unnecessary personal information. Do not continue a sensitive line after discomfort or withdrawal of consent. Do not promise anonymity, deletion, access limits, or product changes unless those promises are operationally true.

### Pitching

Do not explain why the idea is clever, correct misunderstandings immediately, or enumerate benefits. If a concept must be shown, use a consistent neutral description.

### Defending

Do not explain constraints or justify existing behavior. Ask what the participant expected and what they did next.

### Teaching

In discovery interviews, teaching destroys evidence about current understanding. In usability tests, help only according to the test protocol and record when it was needed.

### Interviewing the roadmap

Do not collect votes. Learn the situation, outcome, constraint, and consequence; let the team generate solutions later.

## Remote sessions

- Confirm the participant can access the call and any stimulus.
- Explain what is visible or recorded.
- Provide a way to stop screen sharing before opening private material.
- Avoid asking for passwords, payment details, customer data, or confidential documents.
- Have a fallback if screen sharing or prototype access fails.

## Closing

- Ask whether anything important was missed.
- Explain what happens next without promising a product change.
- Confirm follow-up permission separately from general participation.
- Thank the participant and explain the incentive process.
- Reserve five minutes immediately afterward for debrief.

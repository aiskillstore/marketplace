# Question design and linting

## Contents

- Guide construction and participant-facing translation
- Preflight rejection checklist
- Question hierarchy and lint categories
- Timing, concept, pricing, and review rules

## Build a guide in seven passes

1. Write the internal research questions that the product decision depends on.
2. Draft possible participant prompts without judging them yet.
3. Broaden closed or confirming prompts into open invitations to describe a real event.
4. Map every core prompt back to a research question; remove orphan questions and fill genuine gaps.
5. Put the conversation in a natural order: easy context first, then the event chronologically, then reflection or concept reaction.
6. Keep only a few core prompts and nest clarification probes beneath them.
7. Pilot for comprehension, order, missing coverage, and realistic timing before running the full round.

Treat the guide as a flexible map, not a script. Preserve the learning objective while following relevant, unexpected details.

## Preflight: questions to reject or rewrite

Reject or rewrite any participant-facing question that:

- asks for two or more answers at once;
- supplies the expected answer, emotion, problem, or value judgment;
- presumes the participant did something, knows a term, or experienced a problem;
- asks what they would do, buy, or use instead of what they recently did;
- asks for a typical average when a recent event or bounded timeframe is available;
- uses internal terminology, acronyms, technical jargon, or the team's category language;
- uses a negative, double negative, vague quantifier, or unnecessarily complex sentence;
- forces a choice between options the participant did not introduce;
- names features, competitors, or explanations before open recall, thereby seeding the answer;
- asks the participant to approve, prioritize, or design the solution;
- requests sensitive or confidential detail that is not necessary for the research decision.

## Separate internal and participant-facing questions

An internal research question names what the team needs to learn. A participant-facing question prompts an experience that can supply evidence without exposing the team's desired answer.

| Internal question | Do not ask directly | Better participant prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Why do users churn after one project? | Why did you churn? | Walk me through what happened from your last useful action until you stopped returning. |
| Is onboarding too complicated? | Was onboarding confusing? | Starting when you signed up, what did you do first? What happened next? |
| Do freelancers need AI invoicing? | Would you use an AI invoice tool? | Tell me about the last invoice you created, beginning with what triggered it. |
| Which feature should we build? | What should we build next? | Where did your current process break down, and what did you do then? |
| Will customers pay $20? | Would $20 be affordable? | What do you currently pay or give up to handle this? Who approves that spend? |

Map every core question to an internal research question. Remove questions with no decision-relevant purpose.

## Question hierarchy

Use three levels:

1. **Story anchor:** opens a specific recent event.
2. **Sequence prompts:** keep the participant reconstructing what happened.
3. **Targeted probes:** clarify decision, context, emotion, consequence, alternative, or artifact.

Do not promote every probe into a core question.

## Lint categories

### Leading or confirming

Bad: “How frustrating was the manual process?”

Better: “What was that process like?” then “Was any part difficult or easy?”

### Hypothetical future intent

Bad: “Would you use this every week?”

Better: “How often did this situation occur in the last month?” Validate future use separately through actual retained behavior.

### Approval seeking

Bad: “Do you like our new AI assistant?”

Better: “What do you think this would do?” and “Where, if anywhere, might it have fit in the situation you described?”

### Asking participants to design

Bad: “What features should we build?”

Better: “What were you trying to accomplish when you got stuck?” Product teams own the solution.

### Double-barrelled

Bad: “Was setup fast and easy?”

Better: reconstruct setup, then probe a single moment.

### Stacked questions and probe bursts

Bad: “What were you doing, who was involved, why did you choose it, and what happened next?”

Better: ask one story anchor, listen, then choose one follow-up based on the answer. A list of good probes becomes a bad question when spoken all at once.

### Loaded or emotionally primed wording

Bad: “How much did the confusing setup slow you down?”

Better: “What happened during setup?” then, if needed, “Was anything difficult, easy, or unexpected?” Do not insert intensity or emotion the participant has not expressed.

### Jargon and team language

Bad: “How did the AI ideation workflow affect design handoff?”

Better: use the participant's words, or ask concretely: “What happened after you made that?” A familiar internal term may have a different or unknown meaning to the participant.

### Negative or cognitively tangled wording

Bad: “Was there anything you did not find unintuitive?”

Better: “What, if anything, was unclear?” Prefer short, affirmative sentences and one reference period.

### Forced choices and false binaries

Bad: “Did you choose it for speed or quality?”

Better: “What mattered when you chose it?” Ask about named factors only after the participant answers openly.

### Answer seeding and order effects

Bad: list possible tools or reasons, then ask which ones applied.

Better: ask for unaided recall first: “What else did you consider?” Probe a named option later only when the research requires it. Earlier wording can shape what participants remember or mention next.

### Unbounded recall and invented averages

Bad: “How often do you usually do this?”

Better: begin with the most recent instance or a bounded period: “When was the last time?” or “How many times in the past 30 days, if you can estimate?” Prefer product records for exact frequency.

### Vague generalization

Bad: “How do you usually plan projects?”

Better: “Tell me about the last project you planned, beginning when you learned it was happening.”

### Presuming a problem

Bad: “What is the hardest part?” when the task may not be hard.

Better: first learn the sequence, then ask “Was any part difficult, uncertain, or unexpectedly easy?”

### Unproductive why

“Why?” is not forbidden, but it can invite a neat explanation after the fact. Prefer “How did you decide?”, “What were you comparing?”, “What happened just before that?”, or “What made that matter?” when sequence or evidence is the goal.

## Time-budget test

Estimate 4 to 6 minutes for a story anchor with probes. For a 30-minute session, use 4 to 6 core questions total, including warm-up and close. If the guide cannot survive a five-minute detour, it is over capacity.

Label optional probes. Mark one or two questions as must-cover so a novice moderator knows what to protect.

## Concept and pricing guardrails

- Put concept exposure after current behavior.
- Use the same neutral concept description across participants.
- Ask for comprehension before preference.
- Connect reactions to a recent story rather than an imagined future.
- Ask about current spend, approval, alternatives, switching costs, and consequences.
- Treat stated price tolerance as weak evidence; use a purchase, deposit, paid pilot, or credible pricing experiment for stronger evidence.

## Review output

When the user supplies draft questions, return a compact table:

| Original | Issue | Rewrite or remove | Why it is stronger |

Then provide the final short guide in runnable order. Preserve good questions instead of rewriting for novelty.

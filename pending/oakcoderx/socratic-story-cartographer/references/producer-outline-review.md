# Producer Outline Review Mode

Use this mode only for full-season or multi-episode outlines that need producer triage, a production decision, cross-episode problem localization, or an executable revision plan. Use the main Skill output for ordinary fiction, scenes, characters, and single episodes.

Read and apply [`long-work-evidence.md`](long-work-evidence.md) before diagnosing a long outline.

## Goal

Compile the Socratic loop into two usable results:

1. a producer can choose retain, local revision, structural revision, or hold without reading the complete loop record;
2. a writer can begin the first revision pass without reconstructing the diagnosis.

Use concrete story language: who is trying to preserve or obtain what, who or what prevents it, what choice is made, and what it costs. Do not let terminology, scores, or tables replace a story judgement.

## 1. Establish the Decision Boundary

Record or infer:

- authoritative source and current version;
- development stage;
- the decision the producer needs to make now;
- decisions and story facts that remain unauthorized;
- scenes, facts, endings, and character complexity that must be protected;
- unresolved production, period, legal, technical, or factual constraints.

Do not ask questions merely to fill a template. Ask only when different answers would change retain, revise, rebuild, or hold.

AI diagnosis, a score, a completed file, a validation pass, or a simulated audience result does not constitute producer approval, author approval, canon promotion, or production readiness.

## 2. Diagnose in Layers

Work in this order:

1. run a season-level loop to identify which character choices actually connect the story;
2. inspect phases or consecutive episode blocks to see how a problem accumulates;
3. inspect individual episodes to locate evidence, onset, distortion, and visible failure;
4. cluster episode symptoms that share one upstream cause.

Do not turn a 26-episode outline into 26 unrelated notes.

For every major issue, run this counterfactual:

> If only the episode where the problem becomes obvious were repaired, would the earlier false or missing cause remain?

If yes, move the revision range upstream. If removing an explanatory passage leaves the character arriving at the same outcome, prefer replacing explanation with a consequential action, choice, or loss.

## 3. Build an Issue Route

Put only decision-changing issues on the first page, normally no more than three. For each issue state:

- `Text evidence`: episode, character, action, and consequence;
- `First appearance`: where the cause begins;
- `Distortion point`: where the audience starts feeling authorial force or broken causality;
- `Visible failure`: where the issue can no longer be ignored;
- `True revision range`: the episodes or phase that must change;
- `Why a local patch fails`: what remains if only the visible failure is edited;
- `Protect`: assets the repair must not damage;
- `Counterfactual acceptance`: the test that would confirm or overturn the diagnosis;
- `Coverage`: which episode range supports the route and which material, if any, remains unread.

Keep text fact, inference, diagnosis, and information requiring author, producer, or external verification separate. Do not use a confidence score to conceal weak evidence.

## 4. Separate Hard Errors

List hard continuity or feasibility errors even when they are not one of the top three structural issues. Examples include impossible knowledge, period or technology errors, invalid legal or payment procedures, dead characters treated as living, and unprepared character entrances.

Hard errors may not change the season route, but they must be resolved or explicitly assigned for verification before detailed outlining or scripting.

## 5. Compile Revision Task Cards

Create cards only for evidence-supported changes. Each card contains:

- `Objective`: the causal problem to remove;
- `Range`: episodes or phase affected;
- `Do not change`: protected scenes, facts, endings, and character qualities;
- `Action`: what propulsion or behavior changes in this pass;
- `Acceptance question`: what the revised outline must demonstrate;
- `Stop line`: new characters, clues, reversals, violence, or subplots this task must not introduce;
- `Dependency`: what must be completed before or after this task.

Default dependency order:

1. why a character must act;
2. how the event can happen in the story world;
3. whether information is clear and the scene is effective.

Do not polish before repairing character causality.

## 6. Producer-Facing Deliverable

Use this order:

### Decision Page

- current recommendation: retain, local revision, structural revision, or hold;
- what can be decided now;
- what cannot yet be decided;
- one to three choices requiring producer judgement;
- story assets that must be protected;
- material coverage and its limits;
- the next human approval gate.

### Decision-Changing Issues

Provide clustered issue routes. Do not repeat the complete episode-by-episode notes.

### Hard Errors That Cannot Be Hidden

List only items that must be repaired or verified before the next stage.

### Revision Task Cards

Order cards by dependency so the writer can begin without rereading the full diagnosis.

### Re-check and Stop

Re-check only what this pass claims to improve and the qualities it might damage. Stop when the acceptance questions pass; do not expand the story to make the review look more substantial.

## 7. Evidence and Authority Boundary

- The complete loops are the evidence layer; the producer-facing document is the decision layer.
- Put the loop record in an appendix or separate artifact only when requested.
- Do not introduce conclusions in the decision document that were not supported by the evidence layer.
- Do not promote a proposed change into canon or imply approval that a human has not given.
- If the producer must reread the complete episode analysis to decide, the decision page failed.
- If the writer still cannot identify the first episodes to revise or how completion will be judged, the task cards failed.

## 8. Completion Check

Before delivery, confirm:

- the recommendation is explicit rather than a list of strengths and weaknesses;
- the document states what cannot yet be decided;
- at least one visible failure is traced back to its true onset;
- one root cause is not fragmented into episode-level patches;
- each task card has protection, acceptance, stop line, and dependency;
- competing explanations, counterfactuals, regression risks, and coverage limits remain visible;
- the result is clearly pending human choice.

If any item fails, revise the deliverable. Do not treat structural completeness as evidence of decision quality.

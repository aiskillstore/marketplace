# Design a story that carries the structure

Start with the claim and its conditions, then build the scene. A memorable scene that teaches a different claim has failed.

## Concrete Story Engine

Use one focal person and a familiar setting. Establish what they want, what competing value or cost obstructs it, the choice they must make, and what changes because of that choice. Typically use 150–500 Chinese characters. Names and colorful details are optional; the consequential conflict is essential.

Map four elements explicitly in **Back to the Theory**:

| Scene element | Theoretical role |
| --- | --- |
| Person and goal | Agent, system, or quantity under study |
| Conflict | Competing constraints, incentives, values, or hypotheses |
| Choice | Decision rule, intervention, or change in assumptions |
| Outcome | Predicted consequence within stated conditions |

Avoid describing a situation and then attaching the concept's name. The consequence must reveal why the mechanism matters. Fictional outcomes illustrate the mechanism; they do not establish its truth.

## Anchor test

Choose one focal object or action the learner can picture in 1–5 seconds. Ask whether seeing it could cue the relationship, not merely the story's mood. “A card with a spending limit” cues extra access with a boundary; “a beautiful sunset” does not. A paired symbol can work when the relationship is the concept, such as two opposite arrows.

Name an established metaphor as an existing teaching metaphor when known; do not claim to have invented it. An original scene can use a familiar metaphor. Do not attribute that metaphor to the supplied author without evidence.

## Domain adjustments

- **Philosophy:** distinguish competing normative frameworks and the author's own argument. A sympathetic protagonist does not settle the ethical question. Friendship, consent, reciprocity, and justice are different concepts even when one scene touches all four.
- **Psychology and social science:** stories cannot diagnose an individual or prove an unobservable motive. State uncertainty and alternative explanations; preserve the scope of empirical findings.
- **Technical textbooks:** keep failure modes, concurrency, persistence, and other stated assumptions. A database transaction is not automatically a distributed transaction; confirm what the source actually covers.
- **Math:** retain definitions, variables, domains, and exact relations. Use the story to interpret an actual worked case; a finite example is not proof of a general theorem.
- **Scientific papers:** tie the lesson to methods, population, measurement, and design limits. An observed association is not automatically a causal effect.

## Boundary test

Identify the most tempting false inference from this particular scene. State what the story omits and what cannot follow. Keep this to 1–3 sentences. If an omitted condition changes the outcome, restore it in the scene as well, rather than burying a contradictory lesson in a disclaimer.

When a story would distort a proof or mechanism, show a concrete worked situation, the exact relation, and a simple anchor; explain why a personified analogy is insufficient.

## Observable transfer

A **Real-Life Signal** is something a person could notice or compare: behavior when costs reverse, a change in queue length, a rejected unauthorized request. State what else would need checking. “They are selfish” is a verdict, not a signal. The later **Transfer Test** should change the setting while preserving the mechanism, so the learner cannot solve it by repeating the protagonist's action.

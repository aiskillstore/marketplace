# Review

**Use when:** the creator already has the product and is just talking about it. A creator video that routes nowhere else lands here.

## What a review has to earn

A review argues for the product. It doesn't just show it off.

Viewers assume the creator was paid to say this. Praising the product harder only makes them surer of it. What earns trust is the sense that the creator would have said so if the product were bad.

So the arc builds on itself: the creator makes a claim, tests it on camera, shows the result, then gives a verdict. Each stage needs the one before it, and the verdict is only worth anything because the viewer watched the test.

## The arc

- **The claim.** The creator is asserting something — what this fixes, what it replaced, what they expected of it, etc.
- **The test.** The product does the one thing the claim rests on. Show it in the state that settles the claim rather than the handling that gets it there, and let that pick the camera angles. Give this the largest share of the duration.
- **The evidence.** The product can take the whole frame here, with the creator only at the edge of it. Show whatever the test produced — the result, the change, the state it has ended up in. **Test and evidence are the pair that grows** when the video runs longer than one storyboard.
- **The verdict.** The creator settles and rules on it. The product is held loosely or set down by now — it isn't the subject any more. It is the one panel that shows the person and their surroundings together.

## The concession

Between the test and the verdict, the creator can optionally give something up — a limit, something it doesn't do, a reservation still unresolved (for example, that it takes longer than they'd like).

- Take it from what the product actually is. Never invent one.
- Never let it land on whatever the video is selling.
- Where the user supplied claims, work around them rather than against them.

A review made only of praise reads as copy however well it's performed.

## Fitting the arc to the panels

How many panels a storyboard has comes from its clip's length. The arc stretches across them,
and where there are more panels than stages, the stage named above as the one that grows takes
the extra.

- **One storyboard** — claim, test, evidence, verdict.
- **More than one** — the claim opens the first sheet and the verdict lands on the last panel of the last sheet. Everything in between is further claims, each one tested and evidenced in turn.

**The finished video has to read as one story, not a set of clips played in a row.** Each claim follows from the one before it — the answer to what the last test left open, or the next thing a viewer would ask. Where the order could be shuffled without anyone noticing, the video is a list.

Never open a later storyboard on a hook, never re-introduce the product, and never test the same claim twice — a second pass at something settled reads as the video repeating itself.

## The ending

The video ends on the last spoken line. No sign-off, nothing pointing at a caption or a link. The ruling is the ending; anything after it turns the review back into an advert.

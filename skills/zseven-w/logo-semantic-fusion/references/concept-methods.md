# Concept methods

Use these as structural operations, not as styles or concept families.

## Check fit before choosing a method

Use semantic fusion only when two useful meanings can plausibly depend on the same geometry. Exit to a pure wordmark, single symbol, abstract mark, or identity system when fusion:

- adds a second reading that the brand does not need;
- weakens the primary reading or required script;
- depends on explanation, color, or tiny detail;
- creates category confusion or an unsafe cultural association; or
- reduces distinctiveness by converging on a familiar category mark.

For critique and comparison, diagnose forced fusion instead of inventing a replacement.

## Build families, not method samples

Define a family by its **semantic pairing or interpretation** plus its **dominant silhouette or skeleton**. Use multiple methods inside a family only as variants.

For example, `initial + product action` in a vertical arrow skeleton is one family whether it uses negative space or a shared contour. A second family must change the pairing or interpretation and the skeleton, such as `brand promise + category object` in a radial module system.

Build at least three families. Vary reading order, mass distribution, open versus closed silhouette, axis, and negative-space strategy. Reject a family when all variants collapse into the same familiar category icon.

## 1. Shared contour

Find a boundary that can describe both ideas. Replace part of one silhouette with the compatible contour of the other, then redraw one continuous form.

Ask whether the profile still reads without internal detail and whether removing either reading changes the same boundary.

## 2. Shared stroke

Let two letters, characters, or symbols use one stem, crossbar, diagonal, or terminal. Make the stroke serve both readings; ordinary overlap does not count.

Align cap height, baseline, weight, and corner logic before adding a container.

## 3. Shape substitution

Replace a component with a semantically relevant shape that already serves a similar structural role, such as a lens becoming a counter or a leaf becoming a terminal.

Preserve the host form's skeleton so the result remains one mark.

## 4. Negative space

Carve the secondary signal from the primary silhouette. Enlarge or simplify any void that closes at the smallest committed size.

Make the outer form work before the viewer discovers the second reading.

## 5. Rotation and reframing

Rotate, crop, mirror, or change the viewpoint of a familiar form until it shares geometry with another. Use the transformation for discovery, then redraw instead of retaining a rotated stock icon.

## 6. Glyph construction

Build letters or characters from product geometry, or reshape product geometry around a glyph skeleton. Share strokes instead of placing a pictogram on top of type.

For multi-letter marks, identify compatible strokes, choose a reading order, build a common skeleton, and add a frame only when it resolves structure.

Do not stylize a script you cannot read confidently from memory. Check authoritative letterforms, reading order, confusable forms, and unintended characters. Keep the design provisional until a fluent reader or native reviewer approves it.

## 7. Linguistic bridge

Search through initials, pronunciation, translation, radicals, word decomposition, homophones, and related actions. Convert a useful language discovery back into simple geometry.

Reject puns that need explanation, misstate the language, or become inappropriate in a target culture. Verify unfamiliar language with a fluent reviewer before finalization.

## 8. Functional scene compression

Reduce a characteristic product scene or action to a few structural cues instead of drawing the product literally. Keep only cues that survive reduction and distinguish the category.

## 9. Mother-shape reuse

Start from one repeated or radial form. Transform only existing parts through crop, stripe, recolor, or local deformation so the edited parts create a second reading while the whole retains the first.

## 10. Bridge object

When two ideas lack direct geometric compatibility, find a third object or action that is semantically adjacent to one and structurally adjacent to the other. Use it to discover one silhouette, then remove literal scaffolding.

Accept a bridge only when it reduces the final object count; never retain it as a third pasted icon.

## 11. Modules, gaps, and outer silhouette

Assign meaning across repeated modules, the gaps between them, and the group's outer silhouette. Keep module spacing and corner rules strict so every channel survives reduction.

## Run the fusion test

Accept an integrated concept only when:

- one stroke, contour, void, or module performs two semantic jobs;
- removing either reading materially changes that same geometry;
- one dominant silhouette and one visual rhythm remain; and
- the secondary reading feels discovered, not pasted on.

If the parts can move apart without redesign, classify the result as a collage. If integration damages recognition or safety, reject fusion rather than rewarding cleverness.

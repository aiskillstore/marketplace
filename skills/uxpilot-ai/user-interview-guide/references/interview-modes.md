# Interview modes

Select one primary mode. Add a secondary mode only when it supports the same decision and fits the session.

## Problem and workflow discovery

Use for pre-product discovery, underserved workflows, or a risky problem assumption.

Learn:

- the trigger and desired outcome;
- the actual sequence, people, tools, and artifacts;
- difficult, slow, risky, or emotionally charged moments;
- consequence and frequency through concrete examples;
- workarounds and existing investment;
- decision authority and constraints.

Anchor:

> Tell me about the last time you [performed the relevant behavior], starting with what triggered it.

High-value probes:

- What happened next?
- What were you trying to accomplish at that moment?
- Show me what you used, if you are comfortable.
- Where did that information come from?
- What did you do when that happened?
- What was the consequence?
- What had you tried before?

Do not introduce the proposed solution unless concept reaction is explicitly part of the learning contract.

## Retained value and continued use

Use when established users return, rely on the product, expand their usage, or keep it in their workflow. This is not the inverse of churn: investigate the repeated situations and accumulated reasons that sustain behavior without assuming the product is indispensable.

Learn across time and contrast:

- what originally triggered adoption and the first meaningful outcome;
- what has changed between early use and current use;
- one recent situation that triggered a return;
- the outcome produced and what happened to it afterward;
- whether repeat use comes from intrinsic value, habit, trust, team practice, integration, accumulated artifacts, or switching cost;
- what alternatives remain active and how the user decides between them;
- a comparable situation where the product was not used or was insufficient;
- what strengthens, narrows, or threatens continued use.

Useful anchors:

> Think back to an early occasion when [product] first became worth returning to. What was happening, and what changed afterward?

> Now take me through the most recent time you returned to [product], beginning just before you opened it.

> Tell me about a recent comparable job where you used something else or [product] was not enough.

Do not ask “Why do you keep using it?” as the opening question. It invites a polished summary. Earn the explanation by comparing the early value moment, recent behavior, actual alternatives, and non-use case.

When positioning is also in scope, add a switching lens: reconstruct what the previous approach did well, what disrupted it, why this product entered, and why alternatives still win in some situations. Separate experienced product value from inertia, stored work, integrations, team convention, discounts, or fear of switching.

## Product experience, adoption, and churn

Use when users activate, stall, adopt partially, abandon, or leave.

Learn:

- the signup trigger and expected result;
- urgency and whether the product was a serious attempt or exploration;
- first-session sequence and first useful outcome;
- last meaningful action and exact stopping point;
- whether the barrier was ability, motivation, trust, missing value, interruption, or dependency;
- what happened outside the product afterward;
- alternatives used and whether the original need persisted.

Anchor:

> Think back to when you first tried [product]. What was happening that led you to sign up? Walk me through what happened from there.

Prepare competing explanations before the session. Never reveal the favored one. Compare recalled behavior with available events, support contacts, and workspace artifacts.

For churn, distinguish:

- **Could not progress:** comprehension, interaction, permissions, errors, missing capability.
- **Did not care enough:** weak urgency, insufficient value, one-time job, lost priority.
- **Could progress but would not:** trust, switching cost, procurement, collaboration, price.

## Switching and alternatives

Use for positioning, category entry, replacement, or competitive strategy.

Learn:

- the old way and what kept it acceptable;
- the event that disrupted the status quo;
- active and passive alternatives;
- search and evaluation sequence;
- choice criteria, anxiety, habit, and influence;
- adoption friction and signs of progress;
- reasons someone stayed, returned, or switched again.

Anchor:

> Tell me about the last time you changed how you handled [job], beginning before you started looking for something new.

Avoid asking for a competitor ranking in the abstract. Reconstruct one real decision.

## Concept reaction

Use only when the team needs to understand comprehension, perceived fit, trust, tradeoffs, or adoption barriers around a defined concept.

Sequence:

1. Reconstruct the current or recent behavior first.
2. Record existing pains, alternatives, constraints, and evidence before exposure.
3. Present the concept consistently and without selling.
4. Ask the participant to explain it in their own words.
5. Connect it back to the recent story: where, if anywhere, might it have entered?
6. Explore missing information, trust, tradeoffs, and incompatibilities.
7. If appropriate, offer a real next step whose completion can be observed.

Useful concept questions:

- What do you think this would do?
- What, if anything, is unclear?
- Where might this have fit into the situation you described?
- What would it need to work with?
- What would make it risky or unacceptable?
- What would you need to see before taking the next step?

Do not treat enthusiasm, preference rankings, “I would use it,” or “I would pay” as behavioral validation.

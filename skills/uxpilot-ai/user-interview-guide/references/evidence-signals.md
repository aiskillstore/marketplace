# Evidence signals and decision discipline

## Evidence ladder

### Stronger within an interview

- a specific recent incident with a reconstructable sequence;
- an artifact, message, document, screen, or workaround the participant can show;
- an existing expenditure of time, money, reputation, or coordination;
- a concrete consequence rather than a generic complaint;
- a decision the participant personally made;
- a repeated pattern supported by more than one example;
- a completed next step such as sharing eligible data, scheduling a pilot, or making an introduction.

### Useful but limited

- a clearly framed perception or attitude;
- a concept misunderstanding;
- a stated constraint that can be checked later;
- a feature request with a recent triggering story;
- an explanation consistent with available behavioral data.

### Weak

- compliments and politeness;
- generic “usually” descriptions;
- feature requests without context;
- imagined future use;
- stated willingness to pay without action;
- speculation about other people;
- an isolated dramatic quote treated as a pattern.

## What interviews can support

Interviews are well suited to:

- discovering contexts, sequences, language, constraints, motivations, and plausible mechanisms;
- finding unknown unknowns;
- generating hypotheses;
- understanding the meaning behind observed product events;
- identifying what should be observed or tested next.

Interviews alone do not establish:

- prevalence or market size;
- causal effect;
- task success or interface usability;
- future purchase, retention, or referral;
- statistical differences between segments;
- that the participant's memory matches recorded behavior.

## Interpret self-report carefully

Interview accounts can omit details, compress sequences, reflect imperfect memory, or bend toward what feels socially acceptable. Reduce, but do not eliminate, those risks by anchoring on recent incidents, moving chronologically, asking for concrete examples or safe artifacts, staying neutral, and comparing accounts with observed behavior or records when available. Treat discrepancies as evidence to investigate rather than proof that either source is automatically correct.

## Triangulation

When relevant, compare interview accounts with:

- product events and session history;
- support and sales records;
- documents, artifacts, or current tools;
- usability observations;
- actual signups, purchases, installs, invites, or retained use;
- survey or market data for prevalence.

Treat disagreement as a finding to investigate, not a reason to discard one source automatically.

## Decision rule format

Define before the interviews:

> If we observe [specific mechanism or pattern] among [relevant participants], supported by [triangulating evidence], we will [next decision or experiment]. If we instead observe [competing explanation], we will [different action]. Interviews will not decide [claim requiring another method].

Avoid universal numeric thresholds for qualitative interviews. Use counts descriptively and preserve who, context, and evidence strength.

## Per-session debrief

Capture immediately:

- participant fit;
- recent event and intended outcome;
- sequence and stopping point;
- strongest evidence and source;
- current alternative or workaround;
- concrete consequence;
- contradictions and uncertainty;
- surprise that changes the team's model;
- follow-up evidence needed;
- implication for the decision, labeled as provisional.

Do not turn one debrief into a cross-participant conclusion. Synthesis should preserve traceability to participants and source material.

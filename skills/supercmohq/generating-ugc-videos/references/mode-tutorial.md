# Tutorial

**Use when:** the video shows how to use the product, one step at a time.

## What carries a tutorial

A tutorial holds attention by being useful. It covers one job done properly rather than a survey of everything the product can do — pick the single task or routine and follow it through to its result.

The viewer is working out whether they could do this themselves, so every step has to be clear enough to copy. That is the test to apply to each panel: could someone watching repeat this?

## Working out the steps

Derive the real sequence first — what a person actually does with this product, in the order they do it. Where the user supplied instructions, those are the sequence and don't need re-deriving.

Number the steps across the whole video, not per storyboard, so a second storyboard picks up where the first left off. Panel numbers restart on each sheet; step numbers never do.

## The arc

- **Setting up** — the creator on camera, recognisable, with the product in frame even if this step doesn't touch it yet. A tutorial that opens on hands alone stops being a creator video.
- **The working steps** — the middle of the sequence, where most of the doing happens. Show each action through to its end rather than stopping at the start of it. **This is the part that grows** when the video is longer than one storyboard.
- **The result** — what the product has produced, with the creator back in frame to land it.

Split the duration close to evenly across the panels. Steps carry roughly equal weight, which is what separates this mode from the others.

## Fitting the arc to the panels

How many panels a storyboard has comes from its clip's length. The arc stretches across them,
and where there are more panels than stages, the stage named above as the one that grows takes
the extra.

- **One storyboard** — setting up, two working steps, the result.
- **More than one** — setting up takes the first panel and the result takes the last panel of the last storyboard. Everything between them is working steps, four to a sheet, numbered straight through.

Where the real sequence has fewer steps than panels, spend the spare panel on the finished result instead of padding with a step the product doesn't call for. Where it has more, fold neighbouring small actions together.

## The ending

The result is the ending. The video finishes on what the last step produced, with no sign-off appended after it.

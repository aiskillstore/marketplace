# Try-on

**Use when:** the product is worn — clothing, footwear, jewellery, a bag, anything that ends up on the body.

## What carries a try-on

Viewers watch a try-on to answer one question: what would this look like on me? Whatever helps them answer it earns its place, and the rest is decoration.

Two things follow from that. The piece has to be seen on a body that moves, because movement is what shows how it actually sits — how it hangs, how it holds its shape, how it catches the light. 

## The arc

- **Before.** The creator not yet wearing the piece — it can still be in hand or out of frame, just not on them yet. Where the two frames would look almost the same — a small piece changes too little of the picture to read as a reveal — this becomes the creator already wearing it, reacting to how it looks.

- **Wearing it.** The piece on now, and the body moving. Fit and proportion are read here, and this takes the largest share of the duration. How far back the frame sits depends on what is being worn: far enough that the fit can be read, close enough that the piece isn't lost.

- **The detail.** A tight frame on the most interesting part of the product — the material, some detail or feature etc. Pick whatever a viewer would most want a closer look at. **Wearing it and the detail are the pair that grows** when the video runs longer than one storyboard.

- **The facts.** The details a viewer still wants after watching it worn — how it actually fits, what it suits and what it doesn't, where they would wear it, how it held up through the day, etc.

## Putting it on is never shown

The moment of dressing itself is skipped — cut straight from not-worn to worn, with nothing in between. It's the jump across that cut that gives the reveal its punch.

## It stays the same piece

Across every panel, hold the colour, the shape and the details — its edges, its fittings, its markings, whatever a buyer would recognise it by. Nothing gains or loses a feature between panels, and nothing is invented that the reference doesn't show — the piece stays the same piece however the body moves.

## Fitting the arc to the panels

How many panels a storyboard has comes from its clip's length. The arc stretches across them,
and where there are more panels than stages, the stage named above as the one that grows takes
the extra.

- **One storyboard** — before, wearing it, the detail, the facts.
- **More than one** — the before and the reveal that follows it sit at the front of the first sheet, and the facts land on the last panel of the last sheet. Everything in between is the piece worn: somewhere else, a different way, or closer in on another part of it.

The piece and the person stay exactly as they were throughout, and each sheet follows from the last, so the finished video reads as one story rather than a set of clips in a row.

## The ending

The video ends on the last spoken line. No sign-off, nothing pointing at a caption or a link.

# Flat lay

**Use when:** the product and its supporting objects are arranged on a surface and shot from straight
above

## What this mode is

Several objects arranged on a flat surface and photographed from directly overhead, camera square to
the surface. Squared to a grid with every edge aligned, the same shot is called knolling.

**In frame:** the product and its supporting objects, all on one plane.
**Avoid:** any angle other than straight down; objects tall enough to break the plane; props crossing
the product.

## Read the product first

Start from the object. From the reference, establish the product's top face — its colour and the
material of whichever side turns to the lens — and who would own it. The surface and the supporting
objects follow from that owner.

## Prompt template

Two parts. The first is fixed and goes into every prompt unchanged. The second is what you write.

**Fixed — include whenever a reference photo is attached:**

    The attached image is the exact product. Do not modify, regenerate or redraw it. Its shape,
    colour, label, text, texture and design all stay as they are, and it keeps the state it is in —
    closed stays closed, open stays open. Build only the scene around it.

**Written — the scene only, in this order:**

**Camera angle:** directly overhead with the lens parallel to the surface, from far enough back that
objects at the edges read as flat-on as the one in the centre.

**Surface:** the material, colour and texture everything sits on. It is also the background.

**Composition:** where the product sits — off-centre on a rule-of-thirds intersection — how much of
the frame it takes, and how much unbroken surface stays open, between a third and two thirds of the
frame. The product should be the largest or brightest thing in the set.

**Props and styling:** which supporting objects and how many, all belonging to one plausible owner,
grouped in odd numbers along a diagonal. They may overlap each other but never the product. Say which
items stand upright rather than lying flat, and how far they rise.

**Lighting:** one directional source only, the side it rakes in from, and colour temperature in
Kelvin. Two matched sources give every object two shadows in opposite directions and read as
impossible.

**Shadow:** how long and how soft, all falling the same way. This is the only depth cue an overhead
frame has.

**Camera and lens:** focal length, aperture, and the whole plane in focus front to back. A blurred
prop reads as a mistake.

**Rules for the written part**

- Say nothing about the product itself — no colour, no material, no parts. The fixed block and the
  reference photo carry all of it.
- **Writing the light:**
  - Where it comes from and how it behaves, never what it reveals. A lighting sentence that reaches
    for a ridge, a grain, a seam, a pore, a scratch or a sheen has stopped describing light and
    started describing the product, and the surface gets redrawn to match it.
  - Position and size, never the fixture producing it. A softbox, strip, dish or stand written into
    a prompt is an object in the frame and will be rendered standing there. A source belonging to
    the scene — a window, a lamp, a candle — is set dressing and can be named.
  - One sentence per source, two sources at most. Each extra sentence is another opening for the
    product to get described.
- Concrete and named: "warm mid-grey seamless paper", not "a neutral background".
- 150–250 words.
- Describe only what is visible from the angle you asked for.

# On-model

**Use when:** the product is worn, held or applied. A person in frame routes the shot here
even when the setting or the crop would otherwise read as lifestyle or close-up.

Treat the person as a delivery mechanism, not the subject — subordinate everything about them to
whether the product reads.

## What this mode is

The product worn, held or applied by a person, to show what only a body can show: fit, drape, real
scale, how it sits on skin. The person is there to prove something about the product, and everything
about them is subordinate to whether the product reads.

**In frame:** a person and the product.
**Avoid:** the person becoming the subject; wardrobe, hair or props that out-compete the product; a
pose elaborate enough to take the attention itself.

## Read the product first

Start from the object. From the reference, establish what the piece is, where on the body it goes and
how it is worn. What the product has to prove — fit, drape, real scale, how it sits on skin — decides
the crop.

## Prompt template

Two parts. The first is fixed and goes into every prompt unchanged. The second is what you write.

**Fixed — include whenever a reference photo is attached:**

    The attached image is the exact product. Do not modify, regenerate or redraw it. Its shape,
    colour, label, text, texture and design all stay as they are, and it keeps the state it is in —
    closed stays closed, open stays open. Build only the scene around it.

**Written — the scene only, in this order:**

**Shot type and composition:** how much of the figure is in shot, set by what the product has to
prove, with the crop falling between joints rather than at one. Give how much of the frame the
product itself holds.

**Model:** gender presentation, approximate age range, build, hair and skin tone, cast toward the
buyer rather than the editorial ideal. Describe in full in every prompt of the job.

**Wardrobe and styling:** the state the piece is worn in — fastened or open, tucked or hanging,
without describing the piece itself — and where on the
body it sits — plus everything else on the person and which of it stays clear of the product.
Whatever stands out most is where the eye goes.

**Pose and action:** an action rather than a held position — where the weight rests, where the gaze
goes, what each hand is doing, and which surface the body is in contact with.

**Setting:** the background or location, kept simple enough not to compete.

**Lighting:** skin wants a large soft source, a hard product finish a small hard one — so name which
is the key. A source large enough to flatter skin smooths the pores off it, so call for visible skin
texture alongside. Give direction and colour temperature in Kelvin.

**Camera and lens:** focal length, aperture, and what stays sharp.

**Rules for the written part**

- Say nothing about the product itself — no colour, no material, no parts. The fixed block and the
  reference photo carry all of it.
- **Writing the light:**
  - Where it comes from and how it behaves, never what it reveals. A lighting sentence that reaches
    for a ridge, a grain, a seam, a pore, a scratch or a sheen has stopped describing light and
    started describing the product, and the surface gets redrawn to match it. Skin texture is the
    one exception this mode makes, and it is called for once.
  - Position and size, never the fixture producing it. A softbox, strip, dish or stand written into
    a prompt is an object in the frame and will be rendered standing there. A source belonging to
    the scene — a window, a lamp, a candle — is set dressing and can be named.
  - One sentence per source, two sources at most. Each extra sentence is another opening for the
    product to get described.
- Concrete and named: "warm mid-grey seamless paper", not "a neutral background".
- 150–250 words.
- Describe only what is visible from the angle you asked for.
